Where we start

Part 1 · covers BATNA

Your best alternative decides how hard you can push

6 min live

BATNA stands for Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. It is what you will actually do if this deal fails: the rival supplier, the other job offer, the in-house build. It is not your bottom line and it is not your wish. The term was introduced in Getting to Yes (Fisher and Ury, 1981), which, as Spangler's account for Beyond Intractability reports, calls it the standard against which any proposed agreement should be measured. The book itself could not be opened for these pages; the summary below follows its publisher and standard secondary accounts.

LiveWhat BATNA is, and what it is not3 min▶

It is an action, not a number. "Sign with Corran Box at 412 and pay 9 to switch" is a BATNA. "Nothing above 420" is not; it is a bottom line someone made up, and bottom lines made up in advance tend to be either too stiff or too soft.

It is the best real alternative, not the average of them. Kestrel has three options if talks fail. Only the best one matters, because that is what Kestrel would actually do.

It is not the bottom line, but the bottom line comes from it. Once you know your BATNA and what it really costs, the price at which you should walk away follows by arithmetic. That is Part 2.

It decides how hard you can push. A negotiator with a strong alternative can hold out calmly; one with no alternative has to take what is offered. The leverage comes from the fallback, not from the voice.

Self-studyImprove it before the meeting, and estimate theirs3 min▶

Improve yours first. The cheapest concession you will ever win is the one you never have to ask for because your alternative got better. Get the rival quote in writing, qualify a second supplier, find out what the switching cost really is. Every one of those is work done before the meeting, and every one of them moves your walk-away price.

Then estimate theirs. Brightwell has a BATNA too: if Kestrel walks, what does Brightwell do with that printing capacity? Sell it on the spot market, find another customer, or leave the line idle. You cannot see their answer, but you can reason about it, and a range with an honest confidence level is far better than no estimate at all.

Do not bluff about yours. Claiming an alternative you do not have works until it is tested, and then it costs you the deal and your credibility in the same moment.

Real world

A constructed case: the quote that arrived first. Two weeks before the first meeting with Brightwell, Kestrel's buyer asks Corran Box, an invented rival, for a firm written quote for the same cartons. It comes back at 412 dollars per thousand. She then asks her own operations team what a switch would really cost in new print plates and quality checks: 9 dollars per thousand. She walks into the room knowing her alternative is worth 421 dollars, which means she can hear 455 without flinching.

Part 2 · covers the reservation point

From alternative to walk-away price

5 min live

Your walk-away price, also called your reservation price, is the point at which this deal stops beating your BATNA. For a buyer it is a ceiling; for a seller it is a floor. You get it by pricing the alternative honestly, including the costs that are easy to forget. For Kestrel: the Corran quote of 412 plus 9 to switch makes a ceiling of 421. For Brightwell: its 360 spot-market price plus 12 of extra cost to serve Kestrel's custom print makes a floor of 372.

LiveDoing the arithmetic out loud3 min▶

Buyer: "If we do not sign with Brightwell, we sign with Corran at 412. Switching costs us 9 in plates and qualification. So any Brightwell price up to 421 beats our alternative, and anything above it does not." That is a ceiling of $421.

Seller: "If Kestrel walks, we sell that line's capacity on the spot market at 360. Kestrel's custom print costs us 12 more to serve than spot work. So any Kestrel price from 372 up beats our alternative." That is a floor of $372.

Say the sentence out loud, in full, before the meeting. A walk-away price you can explain in one breath is one you will actually hold when the room gets tense; a number you only half remember is one you will talk yourself out of.

Self-studyCosts people forget: switching, risk, time2 min▶

Switching. New tooling, new contracts, a qualification run, staff time to onboard a new supplier. Kestrel's 9 dollars is exactly this kind of cost. Leave it out and your ceiling is too low, and you walk away from a deal that was better than your alternative.

Risk. An untested supplier can miss a delivery. If that risk is real, price it in: a quote of 412 from a supplier you have never used is worth less than 412 from one you trust.

Time. An alternative that takes three months to set up is not available next week. If you need cartons in a month, the alternative you can use in a month is your real BATNA, whatever looks better on paper.

Every one of these moves the walk-away price. Getting them right is most of the work.

Real world

A constructed case: the forgotten nine dollars. In a first draft of her notes, Kestrel's buyer writes her ceiling as 412, the bare Corran quote. Her operations lead points out that new print plates and a qualification run are not free. With the 9 dollars added, her ceiling rises to 421. A Brightwell offer of 418, which the first draft would have rejected, actually beats switching. Leaving out a cost made her walk-away price wrong in the direction that would have cost her a good deal.

Part 3 · covers the zone of possible agreement

The zone neither side can see

4 min live

Put both walk-away prices on one line and the overlap between them is the zone of possible agreement, often shortened to ZOPA: every price that beats both sides' alternatives. For the carton contract it runs from $372 to $421, 49 dollars wide, with a midpoint of $396.5. Brightwell's 455-dollar opener sits 34 dollars above Kestrel's ceiling, outside the zone altogether. The reservation-price and zone-of-agreement framework was popularised by Howard Raiffa in The Art and Science of Negotiation (1982) and developed by David Lax and James Sebenius in The Manager as Negotiator (1986); both are reported here from secondary accounts. Who first wrote the acronym "ZOPA" is not settled, so no one is credited with it here.

LiveNo zone is an answer too2 min▶

Each side knows only its own end. Kestrel knows 421 and has to guess at 372; Brightwell knows 372 and has to guess at 421. Nobody at the table can see the whole zone. Most of what happens in a negotiation, the questions, the offers, the pauses, is each side trying to find out where the other edge is while hiding its own.

The zone can be empty. If Brightwell's floor were above Kestrel's ceiling, there would be no price both could accept. Then walking away is not a failure, it is the right answer, and every hour spent haggling is wasted. A negotiator who cannot leave a deal with no zone ends up signing something worse than their alternative.

An opener outside the zone is information. Brightwell's 455 is above anything Kestrel can accept. It may be an aggressive first offer, or a sign Brightwell has misjudged Kestrel's alternatives. Either way, it is not the place to start splitting differences. Session 3 is about exactly that first number.

Before you negotiate a price, it helps to have set it well; Economics, Pricing with the number ↗ sets a list price from demand data first.

Self-studyWhy you should not reveal your walk-away price2 min▶

If Kestrel says "we can go up to 421", Brightwell's rational next offer is 421, or very close to it. The whole zone collapses onto Kestrel's edge, and the 49 dollars of room that could have been shared goes entirely to Brightwell. The same is true in reverse.

That does not mean lying. You can share your BATNA's existence ("we have a firm quote from another supplier") without sharing its exact number, and you can share your interests freely, as session 1 showed. What you protect is the one number that tells the other side exactly where to stop.

The honest alternative to revealing it is to use objective criteria for the price: an index, a market rate, a cost basis. That moves the conversation off both sides' secret numbers and onto something both can check.

Real world

A constructed case: hearing 455 calmly. When Brightwell opens at 455, Kestrel's buyer does not counter at 385 and start meeting halfway, because halfway between 455 and 385 is 420, uncomfortably close to her own ceiling. Instead she says that 455 is well above her alternatives and asks what it is based on. She has not revealed 421. She has told Brightwell that its opener is outside her zone, and made Brightwell do the next piece of work.

Build-along

The deal-zone calculator

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps with a working calculator. It runs the same arithmetic you did by hand: the buyer's walk-away price is the rival quote plus the switching cost, the seller's is the spot price plus the extra cost to serve, and the zone is the gap between them. Every input is constructed; change any of them and the outputs move. Write your answer to each step before you check the model.

Read the defaults and write down the four outputs. Before you look, predict them from Parts 1 to 3; then check each against the calculator.

★ Step 1 · the four outputs (model answer)Buyer's walk-away price: $421 (412 + 9) Seller's walk-away price: $372 (360 + 12) Zone width: $49 (421 - 372) Opening number: $455 $34 above the buyer's ceiling Verdict: "A deal zone exists: anything from $372 to $421, 49 dollars wide"

Raise the rival quote to 450 and watch Kestrel's ceiling and the zone move. Notice what happens to the opener.

★ Step 2 · a weaker alternative for KestrelRival quote 450: ceiling = 450 + 9 = $459 Zone: $372 to $459, 87 dollars wide Opener $455: now INSIDE the zone (455 <= 459) Lesson: a worse BATNA makes Brightwell's opener acceptable. The opener did not change; Kestrel's alternative did.

Put the rival quote back to 412, then set Brightwell's spot price to 420. Brightwell now has a much better alternative of its own. Read the verdict.

★ Step 3 · no zoneSpot price 420: floor = 420 + 12 = $432 Ceiling: $421 Width: 421 - 432 = -$11 Verdict: "No deal zone: the seller's floor sits $11 above the buyer's ceiling. Walking away is the right answer"

Return every input to its default and write Kestrel's walk-away sentence on your session 1 sheet, in a form you could say out loud.

★ Step 4 · the walk-away sentence"Our alternative is Corran Box at $412 plus $9 to switch. Any Brightwell price up to $421 beats it. Above $421, we sign with Corran." Constructed case.

Estimate Brightwell's floor as a range, with your confidence. In the real meeting you do not get to see 360 and 12. Write what you would believe from the outside, and what would change your mind.

★ Step 5 · their floor, from the outsideEstimate: $360 to $390, moderately confident. Because: spot carton prices are published; custom print costs extra. Would move: a busy spot market (floor up); an idle new line (floor down). Test with: "How full is the new line this year?"

Write the one line you will say if Brightwell will not go below your ceiling. Calm, true, no threat you cannot carry out.

★ Step 6 · the walk-away line"We would like to work with you, and we have a firm alternative that costs us less than this. If $421 or under is not possible, we will go with it, and I hope we can talk again next year."

Keep the sheet. It now has both sides' interests and Kestrel's walk-away price. Session 3 adds the first number Kestrel should say, and why where it sits matters so much.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Kestrel's buyer says "our BATNA is 420 dollars, we will not pay more". What is wrong with that sentence?

The BATNA is an action, the walk-away price is the number that follows from it. Picking a bottom line without pricing the alternative gives you either a ceiling too low (you reject good deals) or too high (you accept worse ones). Here the arithmetic gives 421, not 420.

2 · Kestrel's rival quote is 412 with 9 to switch; Brightwell's spot price is 360 with 12 extra to serve Kestrel. Where is the deal zone?

Kestrel's ceiling is 412 + 9 = 421 and Brightwell's floor is 360 + 12 = 372, so the zone is 372 to 421, 49 wide. A forgets both extra costs. C uses positions, not walk-away prices. D confuses the opener with a walk-away price: an opener outside the zone does not mean there is no zone.

3 · Brightwell opens at 455, which is 34 dollars above Kestrel's 421 ceiling. What does that tell Kestrel?

The zone depends on Brightwell's floor, not its opener, so A does not follow. B hands the whole zone to Brightwell. C anchors the midpoint on a number Kestrel cannot accept. D treats the opener as a signal and keeps Kestrel's own ceiling private.
