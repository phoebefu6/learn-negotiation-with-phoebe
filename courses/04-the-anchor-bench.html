Part 0

Where we start

Most negotiation advice is built on stories and small laboratory studies. Both are useful and both are easy to over-read. A bench is the opposite discipline: a fixed set of real negotiations and a fixed set of questions, so that when a claim holds or fails you can see exactly what it was measured on. The negotiations here were collected by Facebook AI Research in 2017 to train dialogue models, which is a happy accident for anyone teaching: every person had private values, every outcome was scored, and nobody was performing for a negotiation course.

🎫 Clear all three quiz questions to stamp your passport measured = counted from the 4,000 rows replayed = a rule applied to the real pools

★ What you walk out with tonight. A measured answer, on real people, to "should I make the first offer?" with the two confounds that shrink it; a break button that proves the anchor link is real and not an artifact of the scoring; and a number to quote the next time someone proposes meeting halfway: on these pools it captures 64.7% of the value that was available.

Part 1 · covers what one row holds

A pool, two private price lists, one chat

6 min live

Each negotiation is a small, complete contract. Two strangers on Amazon Mechanical Turk see the same pool of between five and seven items. Each sees only their own values, set so the whole pool is worth exactly 10 points to each of them. They chat, then each enters the split they believe was agreed. If the two entries match, each scores their points; if they walk away or enter different splits, both score zero.

LiveWhy a game about hats is a fair test of negotiation3 min▶

The task is what negotiation researchers call multi-issue bargaining: several things to divide at once, each valued differently by each side. The designers built three constraints into every pool, stated in section 2.2 of the paper: each person's whole pool is worth 10, every item is worth something to at least one person, and some items are worth something to both. The last two make it competitive: both people cannot get 10. The first makes every outcome comparable across pools.

What it shares with a supplier contract is the shape. Several issues, private priorities, a deadline-free chat that can end in no deal, and an outcome that only counts if both sides record the same terms. What it lacks is stakes: workers were paid 15 cents a dialogue with a 5 cent bonus for a maximal score. Keep both facts in view. The bench is strong evidence about how strangers divide value, and weaker evidence about how a buyer behaves when a quarter's margin is on the line.

Self-studyWhat was taken from the data, and what was not3 min▶

The dataset file holds 12,234 lines: each dialogue appears twice, once from each person's side. Pairing the two sides gives 5,916 dialogues; 39 ended with a disconnect and were dropped, leaving 5,877 completed negotiations. The paper reports 5,808; the released file carries slightly more than the paper counted, and nothing here depends on the difference. The rate of deals in the full 5,877 is 81.1%, against the paper's 80.1%, and the mean score per person is 6.06 against the paper's 6.0. The paper's Table 2 also reports that 76.9% of the agreed deals were Pareto optimal, meaning neither side could have gained without the other losing; on the bench sample the same measure is 77.0%. Three independent figures that match is a good sign the pairing and scoring were done the way the authors did them.

The bench ships a seeded sample of 4,000 of those negotiations as plain integers: the pool, both value lists, the outcome, the split, and the first proposal. No dialogue text is shipped. The data carries a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial licence; derived numbers keep the page small and keep worker text out of a public page.

"First proposal" needed reading the chat. A rule-based reader looks for the first message, from either person, that states a complete split ("I'll take the hat, you can have the rest"). It was tuned on 200 hand-read examples, which found 11 misreadings that were fixed, then checked on 100 fresh examples, all read correctly. It reads a complete proposal in 93.3% of the sample; the rest open with questions, greetings or half-offers and are left out of the anchor numbers rather than guessed.

Real world

The bots that "invented a language". This is the dataset behind the 2017 headlines that Facebook shut down negotiating bots after they invented their own language. The paper is duller and more useful. When the researchers let two models train against each other, the models drifted away from human English, so they held one partner fixed: the goal was bots that negotiate with people in English. A researcher on the team later called the shutdown coverage clickbait (reported in the press; his post was not read for these pages). The paper does report one unsettling behaviour: its trained models learned to feign interest in an item so they could concede it later. The people in these 4,000 rows were never part of that story. They are ordinary workers splitting hats, which is exactly why their numbers are worth reading.

Build-along · the bench

Four thousand negotiations, counted in front of you

★ 26 min · the main event

The bench has three panels. The rows, so you know what is being counted. The opener and the anchor, with the break button. And the strategy ladder, five ways of dividing the same real pools, ending with the friendliest rule in negotiation. Work down them in order; the steps under the bench tell you what to read and what to write down.

Read panel 0 first. Write down the deal rate and the walk-away rate. 81.0% of these negotiations reached a deal, 11.7% ended with someone choosing no agreement, and 7.3% ended with the two people entering different splits, which the task scores as zero for both. That last group is a reminder that a deal both sides remember differently is not a deal.

★ Step 1 · your notesdeal rate: ____ walk-away rate: ____ mismatched splits: ____ points per person: ____ (out of 10)

Panel 1: should you make the first offer? Read the opener's and responder's mean points on deals, then the three-way split of who came out ahead. On these rows the opener averages 7.65 points against the responder's 7.23 and comes out ahead in 49.7% of deals, behind in 34.8%. Real, and far from a sure thing.

★ Step 2 · first moveropener, deals only: ____ responder, deals only: ____ opener ahead / tie / behind: ____ / ____ / ____

Now the anchor. The correlation between what the opener asked for and what the opener ended with is r = 0.55. Then read the two confound tiles: in 40.5% of deals the first proposal was simply accepted, so ask and final are the same split; among the deals where the opener moved, r falls to 0.35. Holding fixed what a balanced split of that pool would give the opener, r is 0.41.

★ Step 3 · the anchor, three waysr, all deals: ____ r, only deals where opener moved: ____ r, holding the pool fixed: ____

Press the break button. It shuffles the first-offer values across chats, so every opener is paired with somebody else's ask. If the 0.55 came from the way points are scored rather than from the opener's own number, it would survive the shuffle. It drops to r = 0.05. Press again to put the asks back.

★ Step 4 · break itr before the shuffle: ____ r after the shuffle: ____ what the shuffle proves: ______________________

Read the walk-away table under panel 1. It answers the fear that a high anchor drives people away. On these rows it did not: openers who asked for 8 to 10 points reached a deal 81.5% of the time, openers who asked for 5 or fewer only 69.4%. Session 3 explains why that is not permission to ask for everything.

Panel 2: walk the ladder from the top. The first rung is the anti-lever, always split the difference. Read its share of the maximum, then click down through the people and the two trade rules. For each rung write the share of the maximum and the gap between the sides.

Optional, for the builders: recompute the anchor yourself. A short Python script reads the same rows the bench reads. The shuffle uses Python's own generator, so your shuffled r will differ from the bench's 0.05; it should still be close to zero.

★ Step 7 · the anchor in Pythonimport json, random, statistics, urllib.request url = "https://phoebefu6.github.io/learn-negotiation-with-phoebe/assets/negotiation-sample.js" src = urllib.request.urlopen(url).read().decode() S = json.loads(src.split("window.NEG_SAMPLE = ")[1].rstrip().rstrip(";")) rows = [S["rows"][i * 21:(i + 1) * 21] for i in range(S["n"])] ask, got = [], [] for g in rows: va, vb, outcome, prop, offer = g[3:6], g[6:9], g[9], g[13], g[14:17] if outcome != 0 or prop == 0: continue # deals with a readable first proposal only mine = va if prop == 1 else vb # the opener's private values ask.append(sum(o * v for o, v in zip(offer, mine))) got.append(g[19] if prop == 1 else g[20]) print(len(ask), "deals, anchor r =", round(statistics.correlation(ask, got), 2)) shuffled = ask[:] random.Random(1).shuffle(shuffled) print("shuffled r =", round(statistics.correlation(shuffled, got), 2)) # 3013 deals, anchor r = 0.55 # shuffled r = 0.01

Read the tag before the number. Panels 0 and 1 are measured: counted from what the people did. The three trade-style rungs in panel 2 are replayed: a rule applied to the real pools with both sides' values known, which no real negotiator gets for free. They show what was available, not what anyone could have reached.

Part 2 · covers the first-offer effect on real rows

The anchor holds, smaller than the story

5 min live

The laboratory finding from session 3 says the first offer predicts the final settlement. On real strangers dividing real value, it does: r = 0.55 across 3,013 deals. But two things inflate that number, and a careful negotiator wants the size that survives them, not the headline.

LiveConfound one: the accepted first offer2 min▶

In 40.5% of these deals the responder simply accepted the first proposal. In those chats the ask and the final split are the same thing, so they correlate perfectly by construction, and they pull the overall r up. Drop them and ask the sharper question: when the opener did have to move, did a bigger opening still mean a bigger finish? Yes, with r = 0.35 over the remaining 1,793 deals.

That is the version of the anchoring claim that matters at a real table, because the deals you lose sleep over are the ones where the other side pushes back.

Self-studyConfound two: some pools are simply kinder3 min▶

If your points sit in the one item the other person does not care about, you can ask for a lot and get a lot, and neither number says anything about anchoring: both come from the pool. The bench measures this by computing, for every pool, what the opener would get under a balanced split (the trade-then-balance rung) and holding that fixed. The partial correlation between ask and final is 0.41. Generous pools explain part of the headline, not most of it.

This is the general move whenever someone shows you a correlation from a negotiation dataset: ask what both numbers share. Here it is the pool. In a sales team it might be the territory, the product, or the season.

Self-studyWho opened, and whether the opener was simply the first to type2 min▶

In 86.7% of the readable chats the first proposal came from the person who typed the first message, and in 82.0% it was in that very first message. The first-mover numbers are therefore close to "the person who spoke first", with one difference worth keeping: a greeting is not an offer. Whoever names the first split anchors the chat, whoever said hello.

The first-mover advantage here, 7.65 against 7.23 points on deals, is an average over strangers who mostly asked for a lot. Session 3 teaches the conditions under which going first backfires, and those conditions, mostly about what you know and what you reveal, are not visible in this data.

Real world

A constructed case: Kestrel Foods and the carton contract. Kestrel's buyer reads the bench and decides to open. She does not open at "everything": she opens at a number she can justify with the rival quote from session 2. When Brightwell's account manager counters, the anchor has done its work in the 0.35 sense, not the 0.55 sense. The buyer plans for the counter rather than hoping her first number survives intact.

Part 3 · covers splitting the difference against the frontier

Meeting halfway is fair, and wasteful

4 min live

Splitting the difference has every social virtue. It is quick, it is visibly even, nobody loses face. On these real pools it is also the most wasteful rule on the ladder. Because every person's pool is worth exactly 10 to them, halving every item gives each side exactly 5, a joint 10, while the most each pool could create averages 15.46.

LiveThe number to quote: 85.9%2 min▶

Click the split rung and read its verdict line. In 85.9% of these real pools there was a whole-item deal that gave both people more than the 5 points an even split gives each. That is the anti-lever's honest cost. It is not that meeting halfway is unfair; it is that in most pools both sides could have done better at once, and the halfway rule never looks.

The honest nuance, because an anti-lever that overstates its case is a lie in the other direction: the split never produces a walk-away, and the people's walk-aways cost them. Counting no-deals as zero, the people capture 78.4% of the available value, well above the split's 64.7%, but their deals-only figure is 95.0%. The split beats nothing; it loses to almost everything that involves talking about what each side cares about.

Self-studyWhy "trade across issues" still is not the answer on its own2 min▶

Giving each item to whoever values it more creates all the value there is, 100.0% by construction, but it can be lopsided: the mean gap between the two sides is 2.65 points. The trade-then-balance rung picks, among whole-item splits, the one that maximises the product of both sides' points. It captures 99.7% of the value instead of 100.0% and closes the gap to 2.21. That is the shape of session 5: create the value first, then divide it in a way both sides can sign.

Both trade rungs assume each side's values are known. At a real table they are not, and finding them out by asking about interests, which is session 1, is most of the work. The rungs show the size of the prize for doing that work.

Real world

A constructed case: the halfway price that cost both sides. Kestrel and Brightwell are 30 dollars apart on the carton price and agree to meet in the middle. Nobody asks about payment terms, which Brightwell cares about a great deal and Kestrel barely at all. Session 5 replays the same deal with the payment terms on the table and both sides finish ahead of the halfway price.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The anchor correlation on the bench is 0.55. Pressing the break button drops it to 0.05. What does the drop prove?

A shuffle is a placebo: it keeps every number in the data and breaks only the pairing. A correlation that survives a shuffle is an artifact. This one collapses, so it is about the ask. The confounds in Part 2 then decide how large it is.

2 · Why is r = 0.35 a better number than 0.55 for a negotiator deciding how hard to anchor?

Both numbers are measured and both are true. They answer different questions. The 0.35 answers the one you face when the other side pushes back.

3 · Splitting every item down the middle gives each person exactly 5 points in every pool. What does it cost?

Even is not the same as efficient. The split never reaches for the trades that make both sides better off, and on these pools such trades existed most of the time. Its one real virtue is that it never ends in a walk-away, which is why the people, with their walk-aways counted as zero, beat it by less than you might expect.
