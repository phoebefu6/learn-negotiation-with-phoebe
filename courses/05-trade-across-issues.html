Learn Negotiation with Phoebe · Session 5 of 6

Trade across issues

Kestrel and Brightwell are 30 dollars apart on the carton price, and the obvious move is to meet at 400. It feels fair. It is also the deal that leaves the most on the table, because price is the only issue where every dollar one side gains the other loses. Payment terms, volume and delivery are not like that: each side cares about them by a different amount. Tonight we name the assumption that hides those differences, trade on them, bundle the trades into one package, and price that package so you can see both sides finish ahead of the halfway price.

Part 1 · covers the fixed-pie assumption

Most people walk in assuming a fixed pie

5 min live

Leigh Thompson and Reid Hastie studied what negotiators believe about the other side before and during a deal. Their 1990 paper reports that negotiators tended to start out assuming the two sides' interests were completely opposed on every issue, which they called the fixed-sum error. The negotiators who perceived the other side's interests accurately reached better outcomes. And many missed issues where both sides wanted exactly the same thing, settling for a compromise neither needed. That second mistake is the incompatibility error.

LiveTwo errors, not one3 min▶

The fixed-sum error is believing every issue is a tug of war: whatever they want, they want it at your expense, by the same amount you want it. On the left of the figure, payment terms look exactly like price. They are not. Paying in 30 days costs Kestrel 3 dollars per thousand cartons and is worth 11 to Brightwell.

The incompatibility error is subtler: assuming the other side wants the opposite of you on an issue where you actually want the same thing, and then "conceding" it as if it were a sacrifice, or splitting it as if it were a fight. Both sides end up with a compromise worse than what they both preferred.

Thompson and Hastie found the two errors were independent: avoiding one does not protect you from the other. So check for both separately. Ask "do they care about this as much as I do?" for the fixed-sum error, and "do they actually want the opposite of what I want here?" for the incompatibility error. The paper is read at source as its abstract; it reports its effects with figures this page does not repeat, and the direction is what matters for practice.

Self-studyCompatible interests that get haggled anyway2 min▶

In the carton contract, both sides want the same thing on quality: Kestrel needs cartons that print cleanly and do not fail on the shelf, and Brightwell needs no rejected batches and no reprints. A quality clause is not a concession by either side; it is a shared interest. Yet in a fixed-pie frame, a buyer will often "ask for" a strict defect clause and a supplier will "resist" it, and the two end up trading something real for something both already wanted.

The fix is cheap: before you bargain on any term, say what you want on it and ask what they want. If the answers point the same way, write it down and move on. Session 1's three piles (shared, different, opposed) are the same check done at preparation time; tonight you run it at the table.

The real-rows version of the same lesson, measured on 4,000 real negotiations in session 4: in 85.9% of the pools there was a whole-item deal that gave both people more than an even split. Most of the time the halfway point was not the fair answer; it was simply the first one nobody questioned.

Real world

A constructed case: the 400-dollar handshake. After two rounds, Brightwell is at 415 dollars per thousand cartons and Kestrel is at 385, 30 dollars apart. The buyer, tired, says "let us meet halfway at 400," and the account manager agrees. Payment stays at 60 days, there is no volume commitment and deliveries keep their 5-day window, because nobody brought those terms up. Both sides leave satisfied. Part 3 shows that both of them left money behind, and how much.

Part 2 · covers logrolling

Trade what is cheap for you and dear to them

5 min live

Logrolling is conceding on the issue you care about less in exchange for the issue you care about more, while the other side does the reverse. It only works when the two sides rank the issues differently, which is exactly what the fixed-sum error hides. Measured on 4,000 real negotiations in session 4, giving each item to whoever valued it more captured 100.0% of the available value, against 64.7% for meeting halfway. Trading first and then balancing the split captured 99.7%, with a smaller gap between the two sides: 2.21 points instead of 2.65.

LiveFind the asymmetry by asking about priorities3 min▶

The bench rung that scored 100.0% knew both sides' values. You will not. You have to find the asymmetry, and the cheapest way is to ask about priorities, not positions: "Of payment, volume and delivery, which matters most to you this year?" "If I could move one thing other than price, what would help you most?"

Priority questions are safer than "what is your bottom line?" because the answer helps both sides. Brightwell telling Kestrel that cash timing matters most does not reveal its walk-away price; it reveals where a trade lives. Answer the same question honestly about your own side, for the same reason.

Then price the trade before you offer it. Write the term's cost to you and your best guess at its value to them. If your cost is small and their value is large, it belongs in the package. If both are small, it is noise. If your cost is large, it is not a trade, it is a concession.

One caution from session 3: revealing your priorities can be used against you by a counterpart who feigns interest in issues they do not care about, so trade priorities a step at a time and watch whether information comes back.

Self-studyPruitt's five routes3 min▶

Dean Pruitt's 1983 chapter "Achieving integrative agreements" lists five routes to agreements that serve both sides. This summary is reported, from an abstract summary; the chapter itself was not read. The glosses below are applied to the constructed carton contract.

Expanding the pie: enlarge the resource itself, such as a longer contract or more cartons. Nonspecific compensation: one side gets what it wants and repays the other in a different currency. Logrolling: each side concedes on what it values less, tonight's subject. Cost cutting: one side gets what it wants and the other's cost of granting it is reduced, for example a volume guarantee that lets Brightwell plan its line. Bridging: a new option that meets both sides' underlying interests, which neither stated position contained.

Logrolling is the workhorse because it needs nothing new: only issues already on the table, ranked differently by each side. The other four are worth trying when the table holds only one issue.

Real world

A constructed case: one priority question. Kestrel's buyer asks Brightwell's account manager which non-price term matters most this year. The answer comes fast: payment timing, because Brightwell pays its resin supplier in 30 days and Kestrel pays it in 60. For Kestrel, paying a month earlier costs about 3 dollars per thousand cartons in financing. For Brightwell it is worth about 11. That one answer is worth more than the next three rounds of price haggling.

Part 3 · covers packages

Offer packages, never one issue at a time

5 min live

Negotiating one issue at a time turns every issue into a fixed pie. Settle price first and payment terms become a fresh tug of war with nothing to trade against; settle payment next and the volume guarantee stands alone too. Each issue invites its own split down the middle. A package offer puts several issues in one proposal, so a concession on one can pay for a gain on another, and the trades can actually happen.

LiveWhy the package beats the halfway price for both3 min▶

The halfway deal is 400 dollars, 60-day payment, no volume guarantee, 5-day delivery window. Nothing but the price moved, so both sides' effective figure is 400.

The package is 392 dollars, 30-day payment, a 90% volume guarantee, 2-day delivery window. Kestrel's effective cost is the price plus what it gives up, minus what it gains: 392 + 3 + 2 - 7 = 390, ten dollars better than halfway. Brightwell's effective revenue is the price plus what it gains, minus what it gives up: 392 + 11 + 8 - 2 = 409, nine dollars better than halfway.

Kestrel's price is lower and Brightwell's revenue is higher at the same time. That is only possible because 19 dollars per thousand cartons were created by the three trades, exactly the 8 + 6 + 5 in the terms table. At 2.4 million cartons a year: Kestrel saves 24,000 dollars, Brightwell gains 21,600, joint 45,600. All numbers are constructed for teaching; the arithmetic is computed in the engine and checked by hand.

The price in the package, 392, is lower than halfway. A seller who judged offers by price alone would refuse the better deal. Judge every package by its effective figure, never by its headline price.

Self-studyDivide the gain, too2 min▶

The package created 19 dollars per thousand. Who gets how much of it is still a claiming question, and this one is split 10 to Kestrel and 9 to Brightwell only because of where the price landed. At a price of 396 (constructed, same arithmetic), Kestrel would gain 6 and Brightwell 13. The trades fixed the size; the price decides the split.

This is James J. White's 1984 point from session 1, in miniature: finding value does not remove the need to divide it, and dividing it is still competitive. The bench showed the same thing: pure trading created all the value with a gap of 2.65 points between sides, and trading then balancing captured 99.7% of the value instead of 100.0%, closing the gap to 2.21. A package that one side feels was lopsided is a package that gets renegotiated next year.

Practical rule: build the package to create value, then use sessions 2 to 4 (walk-away points, the first number, the anchor) to claim your share of it.

Real world

A constructed case: the counter-offer that saved the deal. Brightwell rejects Kestrel's 385 dollars. Instead of countering at 390 on price alone, Kestrel's buyer replies with a package: "392, we pay in 30 days, we guarantee 90% of forecast volume, and you deliver in a 2-day window." The account manager runs the numbers back at the office and finds the package worth 409 to Brightwell, nine dollars better than the halfway price it had been prepared to accept. Kestrel's own figure is 390. Neither side gave anything it valued more than it got.

Build-along

Price the package

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps, on paper or in a spreadsheet. Use your own two-issue deal if you brought one; the model answers use the constructed Kestrel and Brightwell contract, where every cost and value is invented. Write your answer to each step first, then compare.

List every issue that could be on the table, not only the ones already being argued about. Price is one; look for timing, volume, delivery, length of contract, quality, service.

★ Step 1 · the issues (model answer)1. Price per 1,000 cartons (being argued: $415 vs $385) 2. Payment terms (currently 60 days) 3. Volume commitment (currently none) 4. Delivery window (currently 5 days) 5. Print quality clause (shared interest: agree it, do not trade it) Constructed case.

For each issue, write your cost of giving it and your guess at its value to them. Mark every guess as a guess, and write the priority question that would test it.

★ Step 2 · cost to us, guess at themTerm Cost to Kestrel Value to Brightwell (guess) Pay in 30 days, not 60 $3 $11 Guarantee 90% of volume $2 $8 Value to Kestrel Cost to Brightwell (guess) 2-day window, not 5 $7 $2 Test: "Of payment, volume and delivery, which matters most to you?"

Find the asymmetric ones. For each term, subtract the giver's cost from the receiver's value. Large gaps go in the package; small gaps are noise; a negative gap is not a trade.

★ Step 3 · the gapsPay in 30 days: 11 - 3 = 8 trade first, largest gap Guarantee: 8 - 2 = 6 trade 2-day window: 7 - 2 = 5 trade Total created: 19 per 1,000 cartons

Build the halfway deal and compute each side's effective figure. This is the baseline every package has to beat for both sides.

★ Step 4 · the halfway baselineHalfway: $400, 60 days, no guarantee, 5-day window. Kestrel effective cost: 400 (no terms moved) Brightwell effective revenue: 400 (no terms moved)

Build the package and compute both effective figures. Show the arithmetic: each side's figure is the price, plus or minus what each term costs or is worth to that side.

★ Step 5 · the packagePackage: $392, 30 days, 90% volume guarantee, 2-day window. Kestrel cost: 392 + 3 + 2 - 7 = 390 (saves 10 vs halfway) Brightwell revenue: 392 + 11 + 8 - 2 = 409 (gains 9 vs halfway) Joint gain: 10 + 9 = 19, the same 19 the gaps created in step 3.

Scale to a year and check both sides stay inside the deal zone. A package that pushes either side past its walk-away is a package that side should refuse, however good it looks to you.

★ Step 6 · a year, and the zone checkVolume: 2,400 thousand cartons a year. Kestrel saves: 10 x 2,400 = $24,000 Brightwell gains: 9 x 2,400 = $21,600 Joint: 19 x 2,400 = $45,600 Zone (session 2): $372 to $421. Kestrel 390 <= 421, inside. Brightwell 409 >= 372, inside.

Keep the sheet. Session 6 takes this package to the close: how to say no to the parts you cannot accept, how to put the agreed terms in writing so both sides remember the same deal, and what to carry into the next one.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Price a package for your own deal with the six steps. If you only had one issue, add at least two more from step 1's list before you start.
  2. Write three priority questions you could ask the other side that would reveal an asymmetry without revealing your walk-away point.
  3. Check for the incompatibility error. Find one issue where you assumed the other side wanted the opposite of you, and write how you would find out whether they actually do.
  4. Decide how you would divide the gain. Move the price in your package by a few units and write down how the split between the two sides changes. Bring the version you would defend to session 6.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Thompson and Hastie (1990) described a "fixed-sum error". What is it?

C. Negotiators tended to start out believing every issue was a pure tug of war, and those who perceived the other side's interests accurately did better. The paper's second, independent error is the incompatibility error: missing issues where both sides want the same thing.

2 · In the constructed carton contract, which term should Kestrel put into a package first, and why?

A. Trade where the giver's cost is small and the receiver's value is large: 11 - 3 = 8 per thousand cartons, the biggest gap. B is a term Kestrel receives, not gives. C is the one issue where every dollar is a dollar for the other side, so it creates nothing. D is cheap to give but creates 6, less than 8.

3 · Why does a package offer usually beat settling one issue at a time?

D. With several issues in one offer, each side can give what is cheap for it and get what is dear to it, which is how the constructed package reaches 390 for Kestrel and 409 for Brightwell against 400 for both. C is wrong: the price still decides how the 19 dollars are split.
