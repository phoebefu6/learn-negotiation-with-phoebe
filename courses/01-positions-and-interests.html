Learn Negotiation with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Positions and interests

A supplier says the price is 455 dollars. That number is a position: an answer to a question you have not heard yet. Underneath it sit the reasons the supplier wants it, cash flow, a busy production line, a bad quarter, and those reasons are where every useful option comes from. Tonight we separate the two, read the four principles of principled negotiation that grew out of that idea, look honestly at what the "Harvard method" label leaves out, and map both sides of a constructed supplier contract that runs through all six sessions.

Part 0

Where we start

Most people walk into a negotiation with a number and walk out having argued about it. The number is the least informative thing in the room. Six sessions build the craft underneath it, using one constructed case throughout: Kestrel Foods, an invented grocery chain, buying a year of printed cartons from Brightwell Packaging, an invented supplier. Every price in the case is made up for teaching. Tonight is about the first move that changes everything after it: asking why.

Part 1 · covers positions and interests

A position is an answer, an interest is the question

6 min live

A position is what a side says it wants: a price, a date, a yes. An interest is why it wants it: the need, worry or goal the position is meant to serve. Two positions on the same issue can only collide. The interests underneath them usually do not, because they are about different things, and that difference is the raw material of a deal.

LiveTwo questions that turn a position into an interest3 min▶

"Why?" asked about a position, not about the person: "What does 455 do for you?" or "What would go wrong at 400?" The answer is usually an interest. Ask it two or three times; the first answer is often another position ("because our costs went up"), and the interest sits one layer down ("we are paying our resin supplier in 30 days and being paid in 60").

"Why not?" asked about your own proposal: "What would make this hard to accept?" It surfaces the interests your offer is bumping into, which you can then serve in a different way.

Neither question gives anything away. Both are cheaper than a concession, and both often reveal that the two sides want different things from the same deal, which is good news.

Self-studyWhere the library window comes from, and what is not verified3 min▶

The library window is the best-known example in Getting to Yes by Roger Fisher and William Ury (1981; second edition with Bruce Patton, 1991; third edition, 2011). Two readers argue over a window; the librarian asks each why, learns that one wants fresh air and the other wants no draft, and opens a window in the next room. A library archive's account places it on page 40 of the 1991 edition. The book itself could not be opened for these pages, so this summary follows that account and standard secondary descriptions; it is reported, not read at source.

You will often see the story credited to an earlier writer. That attribution was not verified in this build, so it is not repeated here. The point of the example does not depend on who told it first.

One interest that is easy to miss is standing: what each person needs to be able to say to their own side afterwards. In business cultures where face carries weight it can decide the deal; Business Chinese Advanced, session 5 ↗ covers negotiation and face in depth.

Real world

A constructed case: the 455-dollar opener. Brightwell's account manager opens at 455 dollars per thousand cartons. Kestrel's buyer, instead of countering, asks what that number has to cover. The answer, after two follow-ups: Brightwell has just bought a new printing line and needs it running full, and it pays its own suppliers far faster than its customers pay it. Neither of those is about the price per thousand, and both can be served in other ways. Session 5 shows exactly how.

Part 2 · covers the four principles

Four principles, one method

5 min live

Fisher and Ury built interests into a method they called principled negotiation, developed at the Harvard Negotiation Project. It is usually summarised as four principles, each one replacing a habit of positional haggling. The book itself could not be opened for these pages; the four below follow its publisher's description and the standard secondary summaries.

LiveThe four, one sentence each3 min▶

Separate the people from the problem. Treat the relationship and the substance as two different things, so that being hard on the terms does not have to mean being hard on the person.

Focus on interests, not positions. Part 1 of tonight.

Invent options for mutual gain. Generate several possible deals before judging any of them; deciding too early locks in the first idea.

Insist on using objective criteria. When interests truly conflict, settle the difference by a standard both can accept, such as a market price, an index or a precedent, rather than by who is more stubborn.

The same book introduced BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. That is session 2.

Self-studyObjective criteria are still chosen by someone2 min▶

"Let us use the market price" sounds neutral until you notice there are three market prices and each side prefers a different one. Proposing the criterion is itself a move. The useful test is whether you would accept the same criterion if the numbers came out the other way. If you would, propose it openly; if you would not, the other side will notice.

In the carton contract, the rival supplier's quote is a strong criterion for Kestrel and a weak one for Brightwell, because it says nothing about Brightwell's costs. A published price index for paper and board, if one fits, is more likely to be accepted by both.

Real world

A constructed case: the index clause. Kestrel and Brightwell cannot agree whether board prices will rise next year. Instead of arguing about the forecast, they agree a base price and a clause that moves it with a published board-price index each quarter. Neither side had to win the forecast argument; the criterion settled it.

Part 3 · covers the method's critics

What the "Harvard method" label leaves out

4 min live

"The Harvard method" is a label other people put on principled negotiation; the book's own name is principled negotiation. The label makes it sound complete. Its best-known critic disagreed. James J. White, reviewing the book in the Journal of Legal Education in 1984, praised its attack on positional haggling but argued that it denies or oversimplifies the hardest part of most negotiations: dividing value that one side's gain is the other's loss.

LiveThe disagreement, held open2 min▶

Fisher and Ury's position: most negotiations are not purely about dividing a fixed amount. Ask about interests, invent options, and you usually find a bigger deal than either side walked in with. The bench in session 4 supports the size of that prize: on 4,000 real pools, meeting halfway captured 64.7% of the value available and trading across issues captured all of it.

White's position: once the bigger deal is found, somebody still has to decide who gets how much of it, and that part is hard, competitive and sometimes adversarial. A method that treats it as a matter of objective criteria and good will leaves the negotiator unprepared for a counterpart who simply holds out.

Both are right about different halves of the same deal. Sessions 2, 3 and 4 are mostly about claiming; session 5 is about creating. Use the label "Harvard method" if you like, but know that it names a method, not the whole craft.

Self-study"Win-win", and who actually said it2 min▶

Getting to Yes is often described as the "win-win" book. Whether the phrase appears in its pages could not be checked for this session, because the text could not be opened. It does appear around the book: the third edition's publisher page quotes the economist John Kenneth Galbraith calling it "a coherent brief for 'win-win' negotiations". So attribute the phrase to its commentators, not to the authors, unless you have checked your own copy.

It matters because "win-win" is easily heard as "everyone gets what they want". The method promises something narrower: a process that looks for value before dividing it, and a fair standard for the division.

Real world

The Sinai, as the book retells it. In the Egypt-Israel talks of the late 1970s, both sides' positions claimed the same land. Underneath, as Getting to Yes tells it, Israel's interest was security and Egypt's was sovereignty. The settlement returned the Sinai to Egypt with large areas demilitarised. This summary follows secondary accounts of the book (reported); the history is far more complicated than one paragraph, and the example is offered for its shape, not as a full account.

Build-along

Map both sides of the carton contract

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps, on paper or in a document. You play Kestrel's buyer, preparing for the first real conversation with Brightwell. Everything about both companies is constructed; the model answers in each box are one good version, not the only one. Write yours first, then compare.

Write each side's position in one line. Use only what each side has said out loud. Do not guess motives yet.

★ Step 1 · positions (model answer)Brightwell: "455 dollars per thousand cartons", now "415". Kestrel: "385 dollars per thousand cartons". Constructed case. Both numbers are positions, not interests.

Ask why of your own position, three times. It is easier to start with your own side: you know the answers. Write each layer.

★ Step 2 · Kestrel, why x3Why 385? Our category margin target assumes it. Why that target? Packaging is 4 points of the store-brand margin. Why does it bite? We hold three weeks of cartons in back rooms we need for stock. Interests: margin, AND storage space.

Now guess Brightwell's layers, and mark each as a guess. You cannot see their interests; you can form hypotheses and plan the questions that test them.

★ Step 3 · Brightwell, hypothesesWhy 415? (guess) the new print line has to earn back its cost. Why now? (guess) they pay suppliers in 30 days, get paid in 60. Test with: "What would make 60-day terms hard for you?" "How full is the new line this year?"

Sort the interests into three piles: shared (both want it), different (each cares about something the other does not), opposed (one's gain is the other's loss). The different pile is where trades live.

★ Step 4 · three pilesShared: a reliable 12-month supply; no quality failures. Different: Brightwell cares about cash timing and line utilisation; Kestrel cares about back-room storage. Opposed: the price per thousand.

Invent three options that serve a "different" interest, without judging them yet. Each should cost you little and be worth more to them, or the reverse.

★ Step 5 · options, not yet judged1. Kestrel pays in 30 days instead of 60. 2. Kestrel guarantees a minimum volume so the line is planned. 3. Brightwell delivers in a 2-day window, so Kestrel stores less.

Name one objective criterion for the opposed issue, one you would accept even if it moved against you.

★ Step 6 · a criterion for the priceCandidate: a published paper-and-board price index, reviewed quarterly. Test: would we accept it if board prices rose 10%? Yes. Weaker: the rival quote alone (says nothing about Brightwell's costs).

Keep the sheet. Session 2 adds your walk-away price to it, session 3 your opening number, and session 5 prices the three options in step 5 and turns them into a package.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Map one real negotiation of your own with the six steps: a contract, a budget, a deadline, a job offer. Mark every interest on the other side as a guess.
  2. Write two "why" questions and one "why not" question you could ask the other side without giving anything away.
  3. Find your "different" pile. If it is empty, you have not asked enough questions yet; write the question that would fill it.
  4. Bring the sheet to session 2, where it gets a walk-away price.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Brightwell says "415 dollars, and that is our final number". Which reply turns that position into something you can work with?

B asks why of the position and why not of a lower number, which surfaces the interest. A and D are counter-positions; C settles the opposed issue before anyone has looked for a trade, and session 4 measures what that costs.

2 · Which of these is an interest rather than a position?

A, B and C each name a specific term the speaker wants. D names the need behind such terms, and it can be served in several ways: a delivery window, smaller drops, a consignment stock. That range of ways is what makes it an interest.

3 · What was James J. White's main criticism of Getting to Yes in 1984?

White praised the attack on positional haggling and argued the method says too little about claiming value. Both halves are real in every deal: creating value makes the pie larger, claiming decides the split, and these six sessions teach both.
