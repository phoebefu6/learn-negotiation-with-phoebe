The first number in the room

Brightwell has said 455 dollars. Kestrel's ceiling is 421. Whatever Kestrel says next will be measured against 455, whether anyone means it to be or not. Part 1 · covers first offers as anchors

Why the first number pulls the last one

6 min live

An anchor is a number that becomes the reference point for everything said after it. In a negotiation the first offer usually plays that role. The counter-offer is built by adjusting away from it, and people tend to stop adjusting too soon, so the final price ends up closer to the opener than either side would have predicted from the merits alone.

LiveWhat the 2001 studies found, from the abstract3 min▶

Adam Galinsky and Thomas Mussweiler ran three experiments, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2001. Their abstract reports three findings that matter tonight:

Whoever made the first offer did better. It did not matter whether the first mover was the buyer or the seller; the side that opened obtained the better outcome.

The first offer predicted the end. First offers strongly predicted final settlement prices.

It held in person and in writing. The effect appeared face-to-face and over e-mail.

That is all these pages claim about the paper. Only the abstract could be read; the full text is closed, so no effect sizes, sample details or settings are repeated here. If you see a confident statistic quoted from this study elsewhere, check it against the paper before you repeat it. For a measured version of the same pull on real data, session 4 finds r = 0.55 between the opener's ask and the opener's final points across 3,013 real deals, falling to 0.35 among the deals where the opener actually moved.

Self-studyPrecise numbers anchor harder3 min▶

David Loschelder, Johannes Stuppi and Roman Trötschel (2014, Social Psychological and Personality Science) compared precise first offers with round ones. Their abstract reports that precise anchors seemed more plausible and pulled the counterpart harder. The intuition is simple: a round number sounds like a guess with room in it; a precise one sounds like the result of a calculation, and people adjust less from a number they think someone worked out.

Two practical consequences. First, if you open, consider a number like 386 rather than 385 or 390, and be ready to show the working, because precision without a reason is a bluff that collapses at the first "how did you get that?". Second, when the other side opens with a precise number, notice that its precision is doing persuasive work on you, and ask for the calculation.

Adam Galinsky's own advice in a 2004 piece for HBS Working Knowledge adds the limit: an opener should be aggressive but not absurd. He also describes an experiment by Thomas Mussweiler in which car mechanics shown a higher anchor price (5,000 Deutschmarks versus 2,800) valued the same car about 1,000 Deutschmarks higher. That study is reported here through Galinsky's account; the original was not read for these pages.

Before you can set an ambitious price, you need a reason for it. Economics, Pricing with the number ↗ shows how to set one from demand data before the negotiation starts.

Real world

A constructed case: the meeting after 455. Brightwell's account manager says 455 and stops talking. Kestrel's buyer, who walked in thinking "around 390", now hears herself say "we were thinking more like 440". Nothing about Brightwell's costs or Kestrel's alternatives changed in those ten seconds; only the reference point did. Her 440 is already 19 dollars above her own ceiling of 421, which is why Part 3 is about what to think while the other side is still talking.

Part 2 · covers the disagreement about going first

Go first, or let them? The research disagrees

5 min live

If the first number pulls the last one, the obvious advice is to always go first. Some of the best-known research says roughly that. Other studies, several by the same researchers, show conditions under which opening costs you. And the most popular practitioner book of the last decade says to let the other side go first. Nobody has settled this, and you should not let anyone tell you otherwise. What you can do is know which conditions you are in.

LiveThe case for opening2 min▶

Galinsky and Mussweiler (2001): across three experiments, whoever opened, buyer or seller, did better, and the opener predicted the settlement. That is Part 1.

Gunia, Swaab, Sivanathan and Galinsky (2013), in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, asked whether the effect is fragile. Their abstract reports that it held across cultures and in deals with several issues, that negotiators with less power also gained by opening, and that it worked through the distributive issues, the ones where one side's gain is the other's loss. The title calls it remarkably robust.

Read together, the argument is: the pull of the first number is real, it is not a quirk of one setting, and the side that sets it usually benefits.

LiveWhen opening costs you2 min▶

When you know less. Galinsky himself, writing in 2004, says going first is a mistake when the other side has much more information than you. An uninformed opener can anchor in the wrong place, including inside the other side's comfort zone.

When the offer gives away what you care about. Loschelder, Swaab, Trötschel and Galinsky (2014, Psychological Science) found that opening with an offer that revealed preferences compatible with the other side's backfired, mostly against recipients who were out for themselves. Loschelder, Trötschel, Swaab, Friese and Galinsky (2016, Journal of Applied Psychology) put it as a model: a first offer anchors and leaks priorities, and self-interested recipients used the leak, even feigning priorities on issues they did not care about. Both are read from the abstracts.

When there is a next time. Maaravi, Pazy and Ganzach (2014, Judgment and Decision Making, read at source) found that anchoring buyers earned more in the single deal, but their counterparts felt worse and were less willing to deal with them again; in a market study, anchoring cut cumulative profit through more impasses and longer talks.

Popular advice. Chris Voss, in Never Split the Difference (2016, with Tahl Raz), favours letting the other side go first. That is his practice-based advice from hostage and business negotiation (reported), not a study, and none of the papers above tests it.

Self-studyWhat the real rows add3 min▶

Session 4's bench reads the first proposal in 4,000 real negotiations between strangers dividing a pool of items. On the deals with a readable opener, the opener finished with 7.65 points on average against the responder's 7.23, and came out ahead in 49.7% of deals and behind in 34.8%. Opening helped, and it was far from a sure thing.

The bench also gives a small, honest look at "aggressive but not absurd". Openers who asked for 8 to 10 points reached a deal 81.5% of the time; those who asked for 0 to 5 points reached one 69.4% of the time. In this data, asking big did not cost deals.

Keep the limits in view. These are low-stakes strangers who will never meet again, paid a few cents a dialogue. The data says almost nothing about Maaravi's worry, the next deal, because there is no next deal in it. It is evidence about the single negotiation, and weaker evidence about relationships.

Real world

A constructed case: why Brightwell went first. Brightwell sells cartons to dozens of retailers; Kestrel buys from three suppliers. Brightwell probably knows the market price range better than Kestrel does, which is exactly the condition under which opening is the stronger move. Kestrel, though, has done its homework in session 2: a 412-dollar rival quote and a good estimate of Brightwell's floor. That homework is what lets Kestrel answer the anchor instead of absorbing it.

Part 3 · covers defending against an anchor

When they open first

4 min live

The same 2001 paper that showed the power of the first offer also showed how to take it away. In each of its three experiments, the opener's advantage disappeared when the other side focused on information inconsistent with the first offer: in one, the opponent's alternatives; in another, the opponent's reservation price; in the third, one's own target. You cannot stop yourself hearing the number. You can choose what to think about next.

LiveThree things to think about while they talk2 min▶

Each one comes straight from one of the three experiments in the 2001 abstract, which is why they are worth memorising in this order.

1 · Their alternatives. What happens to them if you walk? For Brightwell: the line's capacity goes to the spot market at about 360 dollars, constructed.

2 · Their walk-away price. From their alternatives, estimate their floor. For Brightwell: 360 plus the 12-dollar cost of Kestrel's custom print, about 372. You built this in session 2.

3 · Your own target. Not your ceiling, your target: the number you would be pleased with, which should sit well below 421.

Then act on it. Do not counter inside their frame, the way "we were thinking 440" does. Restate your criterion. Put your own number on the table with its justification. And if their opener is outside the zone, say so calmly, so it stops being the reference point.

Self-studyNaming an extreme anchor without insult2 min▶

Naming an anchor means saying, out loud, that a number is not a basis for discussion, while keeping the conversation going. The aim is to remove the reference point, not to win a point about it. Three moves, in practice:

Separate the number from the person. "I can see why you would start there. It is a long way from anything we can make work."

Offer a different reference, with its reason. "The quotes we have for this specification are in the low 400s. Let me show you where our number comes from."

Leave a door open. "If there is something in your costs I am missing, walk me through it." That turns the anchor back into a question about interests, which is where session 1 started.

What not to do: laugh, walk out for effect, or counter with an equally extreme number of your own. The first two damage the relationship Maaravi's study warns about; the third creates a midpoint that still sits closer to their anchor than you think. When the refusal itself is the hard part, Communication, session 6 ↗ covers delivering disagreement.

Real world

A constructed case: the buyer who did her homework. Hearing 455, Kestrel's buyer writes three things on her pad before speaking: "spot 360 + 12 = 372", "Corran 412", "target 390". Then she says: "455 is well above what this specification is quoted at elsewhere, so let me give you a number we can actually work from." The 440 she nearly said in Part 1 never comes up.

Build-along

Open, and answer an opener

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps, on the sheet you started in session 1. You are Kestrel's buyer. Brightwell has just opened at 455 dollars per thousand cartons, outside the zone and 34 dollars above your 421 ceiling. Everything about both companies is constructed. Write your answer to each step first, then compare it with the model box.

Write what you think about first, before you say anything. Use the three items from Part 3, in order, with numbers.

★ Step 1 · what to think about (model answer)Their alternatives: spot market for the line, about $360. Their floor: $360 + $12 custom print = about $372 (estimate). Our target: $390. Our ceiling, $421, stays private. Constructed case.

Write the one sentence that declines to counter inside 455's frame. It should name the number as outside any workable range, without insult, and point to a criterion.

★ Step 2 · decline the frame"455 is well above what this specification is quoted at in the market, so rather than work down from it, let me show you where our number comes from."

Choose Kestrel's opening number and its justification. It needs a reason Brightwell can check, and it should sit inside the zone you estimated, not below Brightwell's likely floor.

★ Step 3 · your opener, justified (constructed)Number: $385 per thousand. Justification: Corran Box has quoted us $412 for the same spec. At 2.4 million cartons a year, a 12-month commitment is worth a volume discount below that quote. Check: $385 > $372 (their estimated floor), so it is credible; $385 < $421 (our ceiling), with room.

Decide: precise or round, and say why. Remember Loschelder, Stuppi and Trötschel: precise numbers seemed more plausible and anchored harder, but only if you can show the working.

★ Step 4 · precise or roundOption A: $385, round-ish. Easy to say, invites "how about 400?" Option B: $386.40, built from the $412 quote less a stated volume discount. Harder to wave away, IF we can show the sum. Choice: B only if the calculation is real and on paper. Otherwise A.

Plan the counter you expect, and your next move. In the running case, Brightwell comes back at 415. Decide now what you will do, so you do not decide it under the pull of their number.

★ Step 5 · their counter, our next moveExpect: Brightwell at $415. Gap: $30. Halfway would be $400. Do not: split it on reflex (session 4 measures what that leaves). Do: ask what $415 has to cover, then move to terms, not price: payment days, volume guarantee, delivery window (session 5).

Write the one-line note for the sheet. One sentence that a colleague could read before the next meeting and act on.

★ Step 6 · the note for the sheetOpened $385 on the $412 rival quote less volume; their floor about $372; our ceiling $421 stays private; on their $415, trade terms, not price.

Keep the sheet. It now holds interests (session 1), a walk-away price and a zone (session 2), and both openers (tonight). Session 4 tests the anchor on real data before session 5 turns the 30-dollar gap into a package.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What did Galinsky and Mussweiler (2001) report, according to their abstract?

C is the abstract's finding: the advantage went to the first mover whichever role it held, and it held face-to-face and over e-mail, which rules out A and B. Nothing in the abstract supports D.

2 · Under which condition did Loschelder and colleagues find that making the first offer could hurt?

A first offer anchors and also leaks information. When it revealed what the opener cared about, self-interested recipients exploited it. B is the opposite of the precision finding; C and D are conditions under which Gunia and colleagues (2013) found the first-offer effect still held.

3 · Brightwell opens at 455. What should Kestrel's buyer focus on to weaken the anchor, according to the 2001 experiments?

Each of the three experiments removed the opener's advantage by focusing on one of the items in D. A and C both work inside the 455 frame, which is exactly what keeps the anchor in charge; B is worth noticing, but it does not move the reference point.
