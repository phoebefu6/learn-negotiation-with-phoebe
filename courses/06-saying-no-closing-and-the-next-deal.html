Saying no, closing, and the next deal

The package from session 5 is on the table and Brightwell likes it. Now come the three moments where good negotiations quietly go wrong: a late request you need to refuse without losing the deal, a close that each side remembers differently, and a relationship you will need again at renewal. Tonight covers all three, separates the famous hostage-negotiator techniques into what was tested and what was told, and ends with an eight-line gate to score your own next deal against.

Part 1 · covers saying no

A no that keeps the deal alive

4 min live

A bare no ends the conversation; a yes you did not mean ends up costing more. The useful no has three parts: say yes to the interest you are protecting, say no to the specific request, and say yes to a proposal that serves their interest another way. It is the same move as session 1, run in reverse: you name your interest so the other side can work with it.

LiveThree parts, said in that order2 min▶

Yes to your interest. "We need a price we can plan the whole year on." It tells them the refusal has a reason, and gives them something to work with.

No to the request. "So an automatic 3% rise does not work for us." Short, level, about the term and not about them.

Yes to a proposal. "What we can do is the quarterly index clause: if board prices rise, the price follows them." It serves their real interest, protection against rising costs, in a way you can accept.

This structure is close to the one William Ury describes in The Power of a Positive No (2007). That book was not read for these pages, so the attribution is reported; the structure stands on its own logic either way.

Self-studyWhen the no will be heard differently2 min▶

How direct a no can be depends on the people and the business culture across the table. Where a flat refusal costs the other side face, a softened no and an open question often do the same work. Business Chinese Advanced, session 5 ↗ covers the softened refusals of business Chinese and how to answer them.

And when the no is really bad news, a price that will not be honoured, a delivery that will be late, the conversation is a difficult one before it is a negotiation; Communication, session 6 ↗ covers bad news and disagreement.

Real world

A constructed case: the late ask. With the package agreed in principle, Brightwell's account manager adds, "and of course a 3% annual rise, as standard". Kestrel's buyer does not reopen the package and does not say yes to keep the peace. She gives the three-part no, offers the index clause, and Brightwell accepts it: the rise they wanted was protection against board prices, and the clause gives them exactly that.

Part 2 · covers closing

Close in writing, before anyone leaves

4 min live

A deal is not done when both people say "deal". It is done when both have the same terms written down. The real negotiations of session 4 show why: each person typed in the split they believed had been agreed, and in 7.3% of 4,000 chats the two entries did not match. The task scored those as zero for both, exactly as a contract both sides remember differently is worth less than no contract.

LiveThe one-sentence summary2 min▶

Before the call ends, say the whole deal back in one sentence and ask for a yes to that sentence: "So: 392 dollars per thousand, payment in 30 days, 90% of forecast volume guaranteed, a two-day delivery window, the price reviewed quarterly against the board index, twelve months from 1 January. Is that what you have too?"

Then send it in writing the same day, with owners and dates for every next step. Anything vague in the sentence ("we will sort the details later") is the issue that will be remembered differently.

Self-studyWho drafts the summary2 min▶

Whoever writes the first draft of the summary chooses its wording, its order and what counts as a detail. Offering to draft it is a common professional habit, and a courteous one: it saves the other side work. It is common practice rather than a measured effect; no source read for these pages measures how much it matters.

The courteous version is to draft it fairly. A summary that quietly tilts a term will be caught, and then the relationship pays for it at the renewal.

Real world

A constructed case: "standard terms". A previous Kestrel contract with another supplier ended in a dispute over what "standard delivery" meant: three days to one side, five to the other. Nobody had lied; both had said "standard" and heard their own number. The two-day window in this year's summary is written as a number for exactly that reason.

Part 3 · covers the hostage-room techniques and the evidence near them

What the hostage-room advice can and cannot prove

4 min live

Chris Voss's Never Split the Difference (2016, with Tahl Raz) is probably the most-read negotiation book of the last decade. Voss had a 24-year FBI career and was its lead international kidnapping negotiator from 2003 to 2007 (reported: public biographies; no FBI page was found). The book's techniques come from that work: tactical empathy, labelling emotions, mirroring the other side's words, steering towards "that's right". They are told through cases. Before you use them on a supplier, it helps to know which were ever tested, and on what.

LiveAnecdote, adjacent evidence, and the gap between them2 min▶

Mirroring. The closest study is van Baaren and colleagues (2003): a waitress who repeated customers' orders word for word received significantly larger tips. That is about tips, not deals. Maddux, Mullen and Galinsky (2008) tested mimicry in negotiation itself, but of mannerisms, not words: in their second study, 10 of 15 pairs with a mimicking buyer reached a deal against 2 of 16 without, and trust carried the effect.

Labelling. Lieberman and colleagues (2007) found that naming the emotion in a distressing image reduced activity in the amygdala, a brain region involved in emotional response. A laboratory finding about emotion regulation, not about negotiation outcomes.

"That's right." No study testing it was found. It is a practitioner's observation from hostage cases, and may well be useful; it is not evidence.

The honest position: these techniques are plausible, some sit near real findings, and none of the studies above tests the book's method in a commercial negotiation. Use them as practices you evaluate, not as proven effects.

Self-studyThe 7-38-55 misquote2 min▶

"Only 7% of communication is words, 38% tone and 55% body language" is one of the most repeated numbers in communication training. Albert Mehrabian, whose experiments it comes from, says on his own page that the formula is about how much a listener likes a speaker, from words, voice and face, and that it does not apply unless the speaker is talking about their feelings or attitudes. Used as a general rule about communication, it is a misquote. Whether any particular negotiation book repeats it was not checked for these pages.

Run the same test on any number a negotiation trainer gives you: what was measured, on whom, and does it apply to the situation you are in?

Self-studyThe relationship is part of the deal2 min▶

Maaravi, Pazy and Ganzach (2014) found that buyers who anchored aggressively earned more in a single deal, but their counterparts felt worse about their own results and were less willing to deal with them again; in a market study, anchoring cut cumulative profit through more impasses and longer negotiations. Kestrel will renegotiate with Brightwell next year. What the buyer learned this year about Brightwell's interests, cash timing and a full line, is worth more at renewal than the last two dollars squeezed out today.

Real world

Chameleons, measured. In Maddux, Mullen and Galinsky's 2008 study, negotiators who subtly mimicked their counterpart's posture and mannerisms reached a deal far more often, and the gain did not come out of the other side's pocket: both did better. It is the strongest negotiation evidence near the mirroring advice, and it is about trust built through attention, which is a reasonable way to read the hostage-room techniques too.

Part 4 · the final scorecard

Eight checks before you sign

3 min live

Each check is one session's habit, stated as something that is either true of your deal or not yet. Score the negotiation you are in right now, not the one you plan to run better next time.

I have written down both sides' positions and the interests under them, and every interest on their side is marked as a guess until they confirmed it. Session 1. Positions collide on one issue; interests usually do not, and the difference pile is where the trades come from.
My walk-away price is written down, with the arithmetic from my best alternative that produces it. Session 2. In the constructed case: a 412-dollar rival quote plus a 9-dollar switching cost gives a 421-dollar ceiling.
I have an estimate of their walk-away price as a range, and I know what in it is a guess. Session 2. The zone between the two walk-away prices is invisible to both sides; an estimate is how you know whether an opener is inside it.
I chose whether to make the first offer for a reason I can say out loud: what I know about the zone, and what my offer would reveal. Session 3. The research disagrees about going first; the conditions it agrees on are information and what the offer leaks.
My first number is justified by a criterion the other side could check, not only by what I would like. Sessions 3 and 4. On 3,013 real deals the opening ask and the opener's final points correlate at r = 0.55, and at 0.35 where the opener had to move.
I offered at least two issues together as a package instead of settling them one at a time. Sessions 4 and 5. Meeting halfway captured 64.7% of the value 4,000 real pools could create; trading across issues captured all of it.
Every no I gave named my interest and came with a proposal. Session 6. A bare no ends the conversation; a no with a proposal is the next offer.
The terms are summarised in writing, with owners and dates, and both sides have confirmed the same summary. Session 6. In 7.3% of 4,000 real negotiations the two sides typed different splits and both scored zero.
Build-along

Close the carton contract

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps, written. You are Kestrel's buyer on the final call. Everything about both companies is constructed; the model answers are one good version.

Write the three-part no to Brightwell's late request for an automatic 3% annual rise. Yes to your interest, no to the request, yes to a proposal.

★ Step 1 · the no (model answer)"We need a price we can plan the whole year on, so an automatic 3% rise does not work for us. What we can do is review the price each quarter against the published board index, so if your costs rise, the price follows."

Say the whole deal back in one sentence and ask for a yes to that sentence.

★ Step 2 · the one-sentence close"So: 392 dollars per thousand cartons, payment in 30 days, 90% of forecast volume guaranteed, a two-day delivery window, the price reviewed quarterly against the board index, for twelve months from 1 January. Is that what you have too?"

List every next step with an owner and a date. A next step without a date is a hope.

★ Step 3 · owners and dates (constructed)Kestrel buyer: send the written summary today, by 5 pm Brightwell account: confirm the summary, or mark changes, by Thursday Kestrel legal: draft the contract from the summary, by the 15th Both: sign by the 22nd; first delivery 1 January

Write the summary email. Short, numbers as numbers, no "standard" anything.

★ Step 4 · the summary emailSubject: Carton supply 2027 - agreed terms for confirmation Thank you for today. As agreed: - Price: $392 per 1,000 cartons, reviewed quarterly against the board index - Payment: 30 days from invoice - Volume: Kestrel guarantees 90% of the monthly forecast - Delivery: within a 2-day window of the agreed date - Term: 12 months from 1 January Please confirm, or mark any change, by Thursday.

Read your email as Brightwell would. Find every word that could be remembered two ways and replace it with a number or a definition.

★ Step 5 · the two-ways check"reviewed quarterly against the board index" which index? which month's value? up and down, or up only? Fix: "adjusted each quarter by the change in [named index], up or down, using the last published monthly value before the quarter starts"

Write the note for next year's renewal. What you learned about their interests is the most valuable thing this negotiation produced.

★ Step 6 · the renewal noteBrightwell's interests (confirmed): cash timing; a full new print line. What worked: payment terms and a volume floor traded for price. What to test next year: would a longer term be worth a lower price to them? Our walk-away last year: $421. Re-check the rival quote before we talk.

Score the gate on this deal. Run the eight checks in Part 4 against the carton contract as you have built it across six sessions. If any row is below 2, the session it names is the next thing to reread.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Brightwell asks for an automatic 3% annual rise. Which answer is a three-part no?

C names the interest, refuses the request and proposes another way to serve their interest, which was protection against rising costs. A ends the conversation, B gives away value to avoid discomfort, and D attacks instead of proposing.

2 · In 7.3% of the 4,000 real negotiations on the session 4 bench, what happened, and what does it teach?

Walk-aways were a separate 11.7%. The 7.3% are chats where both people believed they had a deal and recorded different ones. A one-sentence summary and a written confirmation are the cheapest insurance in negotiation.

3 · What does van Baaren and colleagues' 2003 "Mimicry for money" study show about mirroring in negotiation?

The closest negotiation evidence is Maddux, Mullen and Galinsky (2008), and it concerns mimicking mannerisms, not words. Adjacent evidence can make a technique plausible; it does not prove it works at your table.
